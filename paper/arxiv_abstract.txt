When an agent handling a payment exception loses money, published counterfactual attribution will tell you which of its steps to blame. It will tell you that even when the answer is that a settlement message was dropped and the agent never had a chance. Methods in this line intervene on the agent's own steps--replace a step, re-run forward, measure the shift--and their causal models contain no variable for the environment, so every dollar they explain is charged to a decision. We take a benchmark whose fault process is explicit and replayable, decompose each episode's realised delivery schedule into named, individually repairable messages, and intervene on both the agent's choices and the infrastructure's. A telescoping identity then splits any policy's loss exactly three ways--what the faults cost it, what a better policy would have saved, and what no unprivileged policy avoids--with Shapley values distributing the first term across individual messages. On 545 episodes whose causes are planted rather than derived, across 3 policies, agent-only intervention identifies the true cause in 0%\ of infrastructure episodes, misfiles 100%\ of them, and charges $114,383.40 of infrastructure-caused loss to the agent. Repairing what it names recovers 0.0%\ of the available loss; repairing what a minimal sufficient set names recovers 100.0%, at 2.3 replays per episode against 70.5. Scoring messages one at a time is not merely imprecise: 27.8%\ of episodes do not decompose additively, and on overdetermined ones every single-repair score is zero. Applied without planting, the split says something uncomfortable--for one policy the infrastructure's net contribution is negative, because faults that hide information sometimes stop it from acting on it.
